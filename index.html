Part 1 · Foundations

Level 1Balance Sheet & Foreign Exchange (FX)

The Balance Sheet

DefinitionA financial snapshot of what a bank owns (Assets), what it owes to others (Liabilities), and what is left over for the owners (Equity).

FormulaEquity = Assets − Liabilities

ExampleA bank holds $100 in cash/bonds and owes $90 to depositors. Its Equity is $10. If asset values drop by $5, equity drops to $5.

FX Forward (Locking Exchange Rates)

DefinitionA contract that locks in an exchange rate today for a currency trade happening on a future date, to avoid losing money if exchange rates shift.

ExampleYou expect to receive 100 Euros next month and fear the Euro will fall. You sign an FX Forward today locking in $110, guaranteeing your payout regardless of market drops.

Level 2Overnight Repos & Haircuts

Overnight Repo (Repurchase Agreement)

DefinitionA short-term 24-hour loan where a bank hands over a Treasury bond as collateral for cash today and buys it back tomorrow with interest.

ExampleAt 4:00 PM, your bank hands over a bond to borrow cash to pay depositors. At 8:00 AM tomorrow, you repay the cash plus 1 day of interest and get your bond back.

The Haircut

DefinitionA percentage reduction taken off collateral value by a lender to shield themselves against bond price drops.

ExampleYou offer a $100 bond as collateral. The lender applies a 2% haircut and lends you $98 cash, keeping $2 as a protective cushion.

Margin Call

DefinitionA demand by a lender for extra cash or bonds when pledged collateral drops in market value, restoring their safety buffer.

ExampleYou borrowed $98 against a $100 bond. The bond's market price drops to $90. The lender issues a margin call, demanding $10 in cash immediately to rebuild their safety margin.

Level 3Bond Yields, Pricing & Maturity

The Yield Seesaw Law

DefinitionBond prices and yields (interest rates) always move in opposite directions. When market interest rates rise, existing bond prices fall.

ExampleYou hold a 3% bond ($1,000). New bonds start paying 5%. To sell your older 3% bond on the open market, you must discount its price to $900 so the buyer gets a fair deal.

Maturity & The Secondary Market

DefinitionMaturity is the official expiration date when the government repays 100% of the bond's original principal. The Secondary Market is the open market where bonds can be traded any time before maturity.

ExampleA 10-Year Treasury expires in 2036. You can hold it until 2036 to collect payouts, or sell it early to another investor on the secondary market whenever you need immediate cash.

Paper Loss vs. Realized Loss

DefinitionA paper loss is a temporary drop in market price while you still own the bond. A realized loss happens when you sell early at a discount, locking in a permanent loss.

ExampleYour $100 bond drops to $85. If you hold to maturity, the government repays $100 (the paper loss vanishes). If sold today, you lock in a $15 realized loss.

The 5 Price-Dropping Triggers

DefinitionMacroeconomic forces that push yields up and bond prices down: High Inflation, Growing National Debt, Geopolitical Tensions, Central Bank Rate Hikes, and Heavy Bond Supply.

Level 4Yields, Risk & Credit Premiums

Credit Risk Premium ("Friend A vs. Friend B")

DefinitionExtra interest offered by risky borrowers to compensate lenders for taking on default risk. High yield = high risk.

ExampleSafe "Friend A" (Apple or the US Government) offers a 3% yield. Risky "Friend B" offers an 18% yield because they might go bankrupt. The 18% isn't free money; it compensates for the high chance of getting $0 back.

SummaryLevels 1–4: Everything You've Covered

  • Bank Solvency: Net worth is Equity (Assets − Liabilities). If bond values drop significantly, equity absorbs the loss, creating risk for the bank.
  • 1-Day Liquidity: Banks use Overnight Repos to borrow cash for 24 hours using Treasuries as collateral, while lenders enforce a Haircut to safeguard the loan.
  • Open Market Realities: Bonds trade daily on secondary markets. Selling early during rate hikes forces a Discounted Price and a Realized Loss, whereas holding until Maturity returns 100% of principal.
  • Risk vs. Return: Higher Yield = Higher Risk. Extra payout compensates for inflation or borrower default risk.

PracticeReview Questions: Levels 1–4

  1. A bank holds $500 million in Assets and $460 million in Liabilities. If a market shock reduces its Assets by $25 million, what is the bank's new Equity?
    Answer
    Starting equity = $500M − $460M = $40M. Assets fall to $475M, so new equity = $475M − $460M = $15 million.
  2. Your Treasury desk pledges a $1,000,000 bond into an overnight repo with a lender who requires a 3% haircut. How much cash does the lender hand over?
    Answer
    $1,000,000 × (1 − 3%) = $970,000. The lender keeps a $30,000 cushion.
  3. You buy a 10-year Treasury bond for $1,000 at a 3% interest rate. Rates rise and similar new bonds now offer 6%. If you need cash today, will you sell at a premium or a discount, and why?
    Answer
    At a discount (below $1,000). A buyer can get 6% on a new bond, so they will only take your 3% bond at a lower price that lifts their effective yield to match.
  4. An investor buys a $100 bond. Due to inflation concerns, the price drops to $88.
    • What is the paper loss if the investor holds?
    • What is the realized loss if sold today?
    • How much does the government repay at maturity?
    Answer
    Paper loss: $12. Realized loss if sold today: $12. Repaid at maturity: $100, so the paper loss disappears if held.
  5. Company Alpha offers a 4% yield while Company Beta offers 22%. Why is Beta's yield so much higher, and what main risk does a lender take?
    Answer
    Beta is far riskier, so it must pay a large credit risk premium to attract lenders. The main risk is default risk: Beta may fail to pay interest or principal, leaving the lender with little or nothing back.

ExtraCommon Types of Inflation

Demand-Pull Inflation ("Too much money chasing too few goods")

ExampleBefore a major holiday, demand for toys and clothes jumps sharply. Stores see more people want to buy, so they raise prices.

Cost-Push Inflation ("Rising production costs")

ExampleCrude oil prices rise globally. Delivery trucks and factories pay much more for fuel, and companies pass these higher costs on to shoppers through higher shelf prices.

Shrinkflation ("Hidden inflation")

ExampleA box of cereal stays at $5.00, but the weight inside drops from 20 ounces to 18 ounces. You pay the same price for less product.

Part 2 · Advanced

Level 5Duration & Interest Rate Risk

Basis Point (bp)

DefinitionThe standard unit for measuring interest rate moves. One basis point equals 0.01%, so 100 basis points equals 1%.

ExampleA yield moves from 4.25% to 4.50%. That is a 25 basis point (25bp) rise.

Duration

DefinitionA measure of how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in interest rates. The higher the duration, the bigger the price swing.

Formula% Price Change ≈ −Duration × Change in Yield

ExampleA bond priced at $1,000 has a duration of 7. Yields rise by 1%. The price falls about 7%, to roughly $930.

DV01 (Dollar Value of 1 Basis Point)

DefinitionThe dollar amount a bond position gains or loses when yields move by 0.01%. Desks use it to size and compare risk across positions.

FormulaDV01 = Position Size × Duration × 0.0001

ExampleA $10,000,000 bond with a duration of 5 has a DV01 of $5,000. If yields rise 10bp, the position loses about $50,000.

Convexity

DefinitionThe price-yield relationship is a curve rather than a straight line. For the same size of move, a bond gains more when yields fall than it loses when yields rise.

ExampleA bond with a duration of 7 rises slightly more than 7% if yields fall 1%, and falls slightly less than 7% if yields rise 1%.

Level 6The Yield Curve

The Yield Curve

DefinitionA line plotting the yields of bonds from the same issuer, usually the government, across different maturities, from 3 months out to 30 years.

Example3-month bill at 4.0%, 2-year at 3.6%, 10-year at 4.2%, 30-year at 4.6%. Joining these points draws the curve.

The 3 Curve Shapes

  • Normal (upward sloping): Long-term yields are higher than short-term yields. Lenders want extra pay for locking money away longer.
  • Inverted (downward sloping): Short-term yields are higher than long-term yields. Markets expect rate cuts ahead, and it is often read as a recession warning.
  • Flat: Short and long yields are roughly equal. Markets are uncertain about direction.

The 2s10s Spread

DefinitionThe 10-year yield minus the 2-year yield. It is the most watched single measure of the curve's shape.

Formula2s10s = 10-Year Yield − 2-Year Yield

Example10-year at 4.5% minus 2-year at 3.5% gives a spread of +1.00% (+100bp), which is a normal curve. A negative number means the curve is inverted.

Steepening vs. Flattening

DefinitionSteepening means the gap between long and short yields widens. Flattening means the gap narrows.

ExampleThe 2s10s spread moves from +50bp to +120bp, so the curve has steepened by 70bp.

Level 7Money Markets, Policy Rates & Carry

Central Bank Policy Rate

DefinitionThe overnight interest rate set by the central bank. It anchors every other short-term rate in the economy.

ExampleThe US Federal Reserve cuts its rate by 0.25%. Because the dirham is pegged to the US dollar at 3.6725, the CBUAE normally moves its Base Rate by the same amount, the same day.

Interbank Benchmarks (SOFR, EIBOR)

DefinitionPublished reference rates showing what banks pay to borrow short-term money. Loans, deposits and derivatives are priced as "benchmark + margin."

ExampleA corporate loan priced at 3-month EIBOR + 2%. If EIBOR is 4%, the borrower pays 6%. When EIBOR resets to 3.75%, the borrower pays 5.75%.

Net Interest Margin (NIM)

DefinitionThe gap between what a bank earns on its loans and investments and what it pays on its deposits and borrowings. It is a bank's core profit engine.

ExampleThe bank earns 6% on loans and pays 3% on deposits. Its margin is roughly 3%.

Carry

DefinitionThe profit earned from holding an asset that yields more than the cost of funding it.

FormulaCarry = (Asset Yield − Funding Cost) × Position Size

ExampleBuy a $10,000,000 bond yielding 5% and fund it in repo at 4%. The carry is 1%, which is $100,000 per year, provided the bond price holds.

Level 8Liquidity & Asset-Liability Management (ALM)

Maturity Mismatch

DefinitionBanks borrow short and lend long. Deposits can leave within weeks while loans and bonds take years to repay.

Example3-month customer deposits are funding 5-year mortgages. If depositors withdraw at month 3, the bank still has 57 months to wait for the loan money.

Liquidity Gap

DefinitionThe difference between the cash a bank will receive and the cash it must pay out within a given time window.

FormulaLiquidity Gap = Maturing Assets − Maturing Liabilities (per time bucket)

ExampleWithin 1 month, $200M of deposits mature and only $120M of assets mature. The gap is −$80M, which must be covered through repo, interbank borrowing or bond sales.

Liquidity Buffer

DefinitionA stock of high-quality assets, such as cash, central bank balances and government bonds, that can be turned into cash immediately in a stress event.

ExampleThe bank holds $300M of government bonds it can repo the same day. That buffer covers a sudden $150M deposit outflow without forced selling.

Interest Rate Repricing Gap

DefinitionThe mismatch between when a bank's assets and liabilities reset to new interest rates.

ExampleDeposits reprice monthly while bonds are fixed for 5 years. If rates rise, deposit costs climb immediately but bond income stays flat, so the margin is squeezed.

ALCO (Asset & Liability Committee)

DefinitionThe senior management committee that sets and monitors the bank's liquidity, funding and interest rate risk limits.

Level 9Hedging & Derivatives

Interest Rate Swap (IRS)

DefinitionAn agreement to exchange fixed interest payments for floating interest payments on a notional amount. No principal changes hands.

ExampleA company has a loan at SOFR + 1%. It enters a swap paying 4% fixed and receiving SOFR. The SOFR legs cancel out, locking its all-in cost at 5% regardless of where rates go.

FX Swap

DefinitionExchanging two currencies today and agreeing to reverse the exchange on a future date at a pre-agreed rate. It is used to fund in one currency using another.

ExampleThe bank has spare AED but needs USD for 1 month. It swaps AED 36.725M for $10M today and swaps back in 1 month at the agreed forward rate.

Notional vs. Real Exposure

DefinitionThe notional is the reference amount used to calculate payments. The actual risk is far smaller and depends on rate moves.

ExampleA $50M swap does not put $50M at risk. Only the interest difference between the fixed and floating legs is exchanged.

Options (Calls & Puts)

DefinitionThe right, but not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at a fixed price (strike) by a set date. The buyer pays a premium up front.

ExampleYou buy a call with a strike of $100 for a $5 premium. If the stock rises to $120, you profit $15 ($20 gain minus $5 premium). If it falls to $90, you walk away and lose only the $5 premium.

Hedge

DefinitionA position taken to offset the risk of another position, so that losses on one are covered by gains on the other.

Level 10Equities & Trading Risk

Market Capitalisation

DefinitionThe total market value of a company's shares.

FormulaMarket Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding

Example1 billion shares at AED 10 each gives a market cap of AED 10 billion.

Price-to-Earnings Ratio (P/E)

DefinitionHow many years of current profits investors are paying for. A high P/E signals high growth expectations or an expensive stock.

FormulaP/E = Share Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)

ExampleA share trades at AED 20 and earns AED 2 per share. Its P/E is 10.

Dividend Yield

DefinitionThe annual cash dividend as a percentage of the share price.

FormulaDividend Yield = Annual Dividend ÷ Share Price

ExampleA AED 0.50 dividend on a AED 10 share gives a dividend yield of 5%.

Beta

DefinitionHow much a stock tends to move relative to the overall market. A beta of 1 moves with the market, above 1 is more volatile, and below 1 is calmer.

ExampleA stock with a beta of 1.5 typically rises about 15% when the market rises 10%, and falls about 15% when the market falls 10%.

Mark-to-Market

DefinitionRevaluing a trading position every day at its current market price, so gains and losses show up immediately in profit and loss.

ExampleYou bought 1M shares at AED 10. Today they close at AED 9.60. The position is marked down by AED 400,000 today, even though nothing was sold.

Stop-Loss

DefinitionA pre-set exit price at which a losing position is closed to cap the damage.

ExampleYou buy at AED 10.00 with a stop at AED 9.50. If the price hits AED 9.50, you sell, limiting the loss to 5%.

Value at Risk (VaR)

DefinitionA statistical estimate of the maximum expected loss over a set period at a given confidence level.

ExampleA 1-day 95% VaR of $1M means that on 19 days out of 20, the desk should not lose more than $1M. On the 20th day, losses can be larger.

SummaryLevels 5–10: Everything You've Covered

  • Rate Sensitivity: Duration and DV01 turn a rate move into a dollar loss or gain. Longer bonds carry more risk.
  • Curve Reading: A normal curve rewards lending longer. An inverted curve warns that rate cuts or a slowdown lie ahead.
  • Funding & Profit: Policy rates drive SOFR and EIBOR. Banks earn their margin and carry from the gap between what assets yield and what funding costs.
  • Survival: Borrowing short and lending long creates liquidity gaps. A liquidity buffer and ALCO limits keep the bank solvent through a deposit run.
  • Protection: Swaps lock in rates, FX swaps move funding between currencies, and options cap downside in exchange for a premium.
  • Equity Risk: Valuation (P/E, dividend yield) tells you what you are paying. Beta, mark-to-market, stop-losses and VaR tell you what you could lose.

PracticeReview Questions: Levels 5–10

  1. Your desk holds a $5,000,000 bond with a duration of 6. Yields rise by 0.50%. Roughly how much does the position lose? What is its DV01?
    Answer
    Price change ≈ −6 × 0.50% = −3%, so the loss is about $150,000. DV01 = $5,000,000 × 6 × 0.0001 = $3,000 per basis point (50bp × $3,000 = $150,000).
  2. The 2-year Treasury yields 4.2% and the 10-year yields 3.8%. What is the 2s10s spread in basis points, what shape is the curve, and what is the market signalling?
    Answer
    3.8% − 4.2% = −40bp. The curve is inverted. The market expects rate cuts ahead, often linked to an economic slowdown or recession.
  3. You buy a $20,000,000 bond yielding 5.50% and fund it in repo at 4.25%. What is your annual carry in dollars? What could wipe that carry out?
    Answer
    (5.50% − 4.25%) × $20,000,000 = $250,000 per year. It can be wiped out by a fall in the bond's price (yields rising) or by repo funding costs rising toward or above 5.50%.
  4. Within the next 30 days, $300M of deposits mature and only $210M of assets mature. What is the liquidity gap, and name two ways the treasury desk can cover it.
    Answer
    $210M − $300M = −$90M. It can be covered by repo-ing bonds from the liquidity buffer, borrowing in the interbank market, selling liquid bonds, or raising new deposits.
  5. A company borrows at SOFR + 1.50% and enters a swap paying 3.75% fixed and receiving SOFR. What is its all-in fixed borrowing cost?
    Answer
    The SOFR it receives cancels the SOFR it pays on the loan, leaving 3.75% + 1.50% = 5.25% fixed.
  6. A share trades at AED 25, earns AED 2.50 per share, pays a AED 1.00 dividend and has a beta of 1.2.
    • What is its P/E?
    • What is its dividend yield?
    • If the market falls 5%, roughly how much should this share fall?
    Answer
    P/E = 25 ÷ 2.50 = 10. Dividend yield = 1.00 ÷ 25 = 4%. Expected move = 1.2 × −5% = about −6%.
  7. You buy a call option with a strike of $50 for a $3 premium. What is your profit or loss if the stock finishes at $60? And at $45?
    Answer
    At $60: ($60 − $50) − $3 = +$7 profit. At $45: the option expires worthless, so the loss is the $3 premium.

ExtraCommon Types of Yield Curve Moves

Parallel Shift ("Everything moves together")

ExampleThe 2-year, 10-year and 30-year yields all rise by 0.25%. The curve keeps its shape but moves higher, and every bond in the portfolio loses value in proportion to its duration.

Bull Steepener ("Short end falls faster")

ExampleThe central bank signals rate cuts. The 2-year yield drops 0.40% while the 10-year drops only 0.10%. Short-dated bonds rally hardest.

Bear Flattener ("Short end rises faster")

ExampleThe central bank hikes rates to fight inflation. The 2-year jumps 0.50% while the 10-year rises only 0.15%. The curve flattens and can tip into inversion.
